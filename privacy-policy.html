Privacy Policy for Canter

Last Updated: September 25, 2026

Overview

Canter, formerly RunWalk ("the App"), is a run-walk interval timer available for iOS, watchOS, and Android. Your privacy is important to us, and we want to be transparent about our data practices.

In short: The App never sends us, the developer, your workout records, routes, GPS positions, anything it reads from Apple Health or Health Connect, or Strava account details, and from your settings it sends only whether GPS was on for a workout, and which main switch you changed with its new on or off value. What it does send, on both iOS and Android, is anonymous usage events and crash reports to PostHog, a product analytics service: which screens are opened, when a workout starts and ends with the coarse measurements the App makes itself, such as its duration and distance as plain numbers, and the technical details of a crash, tied to a random identifier created for the installation rather than to your name or any account. You can turn this off in the App under Settings > Privacy > Share anonymous usage and crash reports; what the reports carry is described under Usage and crash reports. Versions of the iOS app released before that switch existed report crashes to Google's Firebase Crashlytics instead, as described under Earlier versions. The iOS app also asks Firebase App Check to confirm that it is a genuine copy, and the Google Maps SDK inside the Android app sends Google its own diagnostics and usage measurements, both described below. Your workouts live on your device and, if you use the platform's own features, in your own iCloud account or Google account. Apart from PostHog, the only places the App itself sends anything are Apple and Google, through the map, health, backup, sync, app check and rating features described below; what your health store or your account does with its copy afterwards (other apps you have allowed, an export you set up) is described below too. Beyond that, the only things that can reach us are what you choose to send: an email to support, answers to an optional feedback survey in the App, feedback you enter while the Android app is in a closed test on Google Play, or a review you enter in a store, which the store publishes and also shows us in its developer console under the name you review with.

Data the App Handles

Location Data

The App uses your device's GPS location only while a workout is running to:

  • Track your running/walking route
  • Calculate distance and pace
  • Display your route on a map
Important:
  • GPS tracking can be switched off in Settings; a distance-based workout needs it on
  • Your route is stored in your workout history on your device, and travels with that history only as described under Where Your Data Lives (your own iCloud sync on iOS, your own Google backup on Android)
  • The App never sends your GPS location to us. While a route map is on screen, the map tiles are requested from Apple Maps or Google Maps, and those requests carry the map area being displayed, which is where you are or were, together with your IP address (see Third-Party Services)
  • Usage and crash reports carry no GPS position, and no location is worked out from your IP address for them, as described under Usage and crash reports
  • Location is read only while the App is tracking a workout, never in the background otherwise

Health & Fitness Data

On iOS and watchOS (Apple HealthKit)

With your explicit permission, the App may:

  • Write: Workout sessions, duration, distance, active calories burned
  • Read: The workouts Canter itself wrote to Apple Health, to keep the app's history in step with them

On Android (Health Connect)

With your explicit permission, the App may:

  • Write: Exercise sessions, distance, active calories burned
  • Read: Nothing. The Android app writes only and never reads from Health Connect
Important:
  • Health data written by the App lives in Apple Health or Health Connect on your device, under their security and your own health-sharing settings (Apple Health may also sync through your iCloud account if you have that enabled)
  • The App never sends us anything it reads from Apple Health or Health Connect. Usage events do carry two measurements the App itself makes during a workout, its duration and its distance, as plain numbers, the same figures the App shows you; they never carry calories, heart rate or your route (see Usage and crash reports). The App itself writes workout duration, distance and calories only to your health store when you allow it, and its history is copied only to the places described under Where Your Data Lives: the sync or backup copy kept under your own account, and a new device you transfer to directly. What happens to the health store afterwards is under your control there: other apps you have allowed can read it, and Health Connect can export it to a cloud provider you choose (Android 14 and later). Those copies are managed, and deleted, in the health store or at that provider, not in the App
  • Other apps you have allowed in Apple Health or Health Connect can read the workouts Canter wrote there; that sharing is yours to grant and revoke in the health store
  • Health data is never used for advertising or marketing

Data the App Does NOT Collect

The App:

  • Does NOT ask for your name, email address or any other account details; using the App needs no identity at all
  • Does NOT require an account or sign-in
  • Does NOT include any advertising SDK and does NOT use your device's advertising identifier. What measurement there is: the App sends anonymous usage events and crash reports to PostHog, as described under Usage and crash reports, which you can turn off in Settings; the Google Maps SDK on Android measures its own use for Google; and Apple and Google may keep their own operational records of their sync, backup, health and Firebase services under their policies
  • Does NOT contain advertisements
  • Does NOT send us your workout records, routes, GPS positions, anything it reads from Apple Health or Health Connect, or Strava account details, and sends nothing from your settings except whether GPS was on for a workout, and which main switch you changed with its new on or off value. Usage events carry only what is listed under Usage and crash reports: screen names, coarse measurements the App makes itself, such as a workout's duration and distance, the identifier of a built-in preset or training plan, whether GPS was on, and which main switch you changed in Settings with its new value. Apart from those usage events and crash reports, nothing the App does on its own reaches the developer; only what you choose to send us (a support email, survey answers, closed-test feedback, or a store review) does
  • Does NOT access your contacts, photos, files or messages

Where Your Data Lives

The App stores on your device:

  • Workout history - Your completed workouts, with their routes when GPS was on
  • App settings - Your preferences (voice, sounds, GPS mode, units)
  • Training plan progress - Which plan you picked and which sessions you completed

On iOS, your workout history also syncs between your own devices through your iCloud account (Apple CloudKit), if iCloud is enabled for the App. Apple stores that copy under your Apple Account, protected by Apple's iCloud data protection under your own account settings, and handles it under Apple's privacy policy. Separately, the App's local history and settings are eligible for your device's own backups (iCloud Backup, or a backup to a computer), because the App does not exclude its data from them; whether a copy exists depends on your backup settings, including whether Canter is switched on within them. A restore from a completed backup brings back the history it contained.

On Android, your workout history, settings and plan progress are eligible for Android's app backup when you have Google backup enabled, within Android's limits on backup size, and in a direct device-to-device transfer when you choose one during setup, which works whether or not cloud backup is on. Google stores the backup under your Google Account, encrypted in transit and at rest; on supported Android versions with a PIN, pattern or password set, it is additionally encrypted with your lock-screen secret. Google handles it under Google's privacy policy. A reinstall with backup on can restore what a completed backup contained.

We have no access to your device, your iCloud account or your Google account, so we cannot see any of these copies.

  • Apple HealthKit and Apple Health (iOS/watchOS) - Apple Privacy Policy
  • Apple iCloud (CloudKit) (iOS/watchOS) - history sync between your own devices - Apple Privacy Policy
  • Apple Maps (iOS/watchOS) - route maps - Apple Privacy Policy
  • Google Health Connect (Android) - Google Privacy Policy
  • Google Maps SDK for Android (Android) - route maps - Google Privacy Policy
  • Android Backup (Android) - your own Google Account backup - Google Privacy Policy

  • Firebase App Check (iOS) - proof that requests come from the genuine app - Firebase Privacy and Security

A rating or review you enter there is collected by that store and published the way store reviews are; while the Android app is in a closed test on Google Play, that feedback goes to the developer instead of being published

Map tiles. While a route map is on screen, the tiles are requested from Apple Maps (iOS) or Google Maps (Android). Those requests carry the map area being displayed and your device's IP address, and are handled under Apple's or Google's privacy policy.

Google Maps SDK. On Android, the Maps SDK that draws the map also collects, for Google, technical information about its use: device model and operating system version, the SDK version, a pseudonymous identifier for the SDK installation, diagnostics if the SDK crashes, and events when the map is panned or zoomed. Google uses this to measure the use of and improve its services, as described in its Maps SDK data disclosure. The App creates the map only when a route is shown.

Usage and crash reports (iOS and Android)

The iPhone and Android apps send anonymous usage events and crash reports to PostHog, Inc., a product analytics service, which holds them for us in its United States cloud, where we can read them. The Apple Watch app sends none of its own; a workout started on the watch can be reported by the iPhone app, marked as started on the watch.

Usage events. A usage event records that something happened in the App: the App was installed, updated, opened (recording whether it was launched fresh or returned from the background) or sent to the background; a screen was shown, by the screen's name; a workout started, was saved or was discarded; a training plan was started, a plan workout was saved or a plan was completed; the store's rating sheet was requested; or one of the main switches in Settings (bells, haptics, voice, GPS, saving to Health, auto-pause, reminders, starting with a walk, and this reporting switch) was turned on or off. Workout events carry coarse facts: the workout mode, the identifier of the built-in preset or plan day (or "custom"), the plan week and day, whether GPS was on, where the workout was started (phone, watch, widget, shortcut or quick start), and, when it ends, its duration in seconds, its distance in whole meters, the number of intervals, whether auto-pause was used, and, for a discarded workout, why it was discarded. They never carry your route, your pace over time, your GPS position or anything from your health store.

Details on every report. Usage events and crash reports include these details where available: the time it happened, a random identifier for the event itself, the random identifier for the installation, a session identifier, the App's name, package identifier, version and build, the name and version of PostHog's library, the device's manufacturer and model, the device category (phone or tablet), the operating system and its version, the screen size, the time zone, and the type of network connection (for example Wi-Fi or cellular). On iOS they also include your language setting (the language only, not the region), whether the App was installed from TestFlight or from outside the App Store, and whether it runs on a simulator or on a Mac. On Android they also include your language and region setting, the screen density, whether it runs on an emulator, the name of the mobile network operator when the phone reports one, and the system's standard user-agent string, which repeats the Android version and device model. The device model is sent as a model name or hardware code, never as the personal name you give your device. Reports never carry your phone number, your Apple Account or Google Account, or your device's advertising identifier. When the App has just been updated, the update event also carries the version and build it was updated from.

Crash reports. A crash report is sent when the App crashes or runs into an error we have chosen to record, such as the iCloud sync store failing to open on iOS. On iOS it carries the kind of error, its message, how it was caught and how severe it was, the number of the thread it happened on, and the stack trace (the list of the App's functions that were running at the time, with their memory addresses). So that the trace can be turned back into readable function names, it also carries a description of each piece of program code involved: its file path on the device, which includes the folder name the system assigns to the App's installation, its build identifier, and where it was loaded in memory. On Android it carries the kind of error, its message, how it was caught and how severe it was, the number of the thread it happened on, and for each step of the stack trace the name of the class and function, and the source file name and line number where available, together with an identifier for the App's build that lets the names be read; it carries no memory addresses or list of loaded code. On both platforms the report also carries the details above.

The installation identifier. The random identifier is created by PostHog's library inside the App the first time it runs with reporting on. It is not taken from your device, your Apple Account or your Google Account. It lives in the App's own storage on the device and is kept out of backups on both platforms: on iOS PostHog's library marks its files as excluded from device backups, and on Android the App's backup leaves them out. Uninstalling the App removes it, and a reinstall, or a restore from a backup, starts a new one. The App never tells PostHog who you are, so PostHog keeps no profile of you, only events under that identifier.

IP address. Every connection to PostHog, like any internet connection, gives PostHog your device's IP address. Our PostHog project is set to discard client IP addresses, so the address is not stored with the reports, and no location is worked out from it or stored. PostHog's library also contacts PostHog to fetch its settings for the App; that request names only our PostHog project, not your installation, and reaches PostHog from your IP address in the same way. When surveys are enabled in a version of the App, the library also asks PostHog whether a survey applies to this installation, whether or not one is then shown; that request carries the installation identifier, the App's version and build, its package identifier, the operating system and its version, the device category, the time zone, and the name and version of PostHog's library.

How long they are kept. PostHog keeps these events available to us for seven years, the retention period of PostHog's paid plans, as its events retention page sets out. PostHog describes that period as the window within which events can be queried, not as a date on which they are erased, so we do not promise that reports are deleted when it ends. We may delete data sooner by deleting it from our PostHog project. PostHog handles them under its privacy policy.

Turning them off. You can turn usage events and crash reports off in the App under Settings > Privacy > Share anonymous usage and crash reports. The switch is on when you install the App. Turning it off sends one last event recording that the switch was turned off, then stops all usage events and crash reports at once; while it stays off, the App does not start PostHog's library at all. Turning it back on resumes them. Reports already sent carry no name or account, only the random installation identifier, which the App never shows you, so we cannot look reports up by name or account, or find the ones that came from your device. The one exception is a survey answer whose exact text you give us, as described under Feedback survey. Email support if you have questions about them.

Feedback survey. The App may show a short, optional survey inside the App, asking what would make Canter better for you and how likely you are to keep using it. Answering is up to you, and you can dismiss it. Your answers are sent to PostHog with the same random installation identifier as the usage events, and we read the written answers ourselves. Showing the survey and dismissing it are recorded as events too, with the survey's identifier, and if you dismiss it after answering part of it, the answers you had given are sent with the dismissal. Please do not put your name, email address, health details or other personal details in your answers; a written answer can identify you if you write something identifying. The survey is shown only while usage and crash reports are switched on. The check for whether a survey applies, described under IP address above, may itself be recorded as an event naming the survey rule and its result, and that result may be attached to later reports from the same installation, even when no survey appears. If you tell support the exact text of an answer you wrote, we can find that answer and the other events sent under the same identifier. PostHog's deletion tools work on person profiles, which these anonymous events do not have, so we will remove them as far as those tools allow, and a complete removal before the retention period ends may not be possible.

Earlier versions. Versions of the iOS app that do not have the Share anonymous usage and crash reports switch send crash reports, and short session reports from which crash-free sessions are counted, to Google's Firebase Crashlytics instead of PostHog, as the previous version of this policy describes in full. Those reports carry the crash trace, device details, pseudonymous identifiers that Firebase assigns to the installation and the session, and an installation token that Firebase issues. Every connection to Firebase also gives Google your device's IP address. In those versions crash reporting cannot be switched off inside the App, and uninstalling does not erase reports already sent. Google keeps crash traces and the identifiers attached to them for 90 days, after which it begins removing them. Versions of the Android app without that switch have no usage or crash reporting of their own; the Google Maps SDK's own diagnostics are described above.

Firebase App Check (iOS). Firebase App Check, on iOS, asks Apple's App Attest service to vouch that the request comes from a genuine copy of the App and passes that proof to Firebase; the exchange carries the app's identity, device and operating-system details, a key identifier and Apple's attestation object, but none of your workout, route or health content. Every connection the iOS app makes to Firebase for an app check also gives Google your device's IP address and the usual connection details, which Google uses to operate, secure and improve Firebase under its own privacy policy.

Beyond these platform services and PostHog (and, in the earlier iOS versions described above, Firebase Crashlytics), the App sends nothing to any advertising network, other analytics platform or other third-party data collection service.

Managing Your Data

Location Permissions

  • iOS: Settings > Privacy & Security > Location Services > Canter
  • Android: Settings > Apps > Canter > Permissions > Location

Health Permissions

  • iOS 18 and later: Settings > Apps > Health > Data Access & Devices > Canter (earlier iOS: Settings > Health > Data Access & Devices > Canter), or in the Health app under Sharing > Apps and Services
  • Android 14 and later: Settings > Security & privacy > Privacy controls > Health Connect > App permissions > Canter (earlier Android: open the Health Connect app > App permissions > Canter)

Usage and Crash Reports

  • iOS and Android: In the App, Settings > Privacy > Share anonymous usage and crash reports. Earlier iOS versions without this switch have no way to turn crash reporting off (see Earlier versions)

Deleting Your Data

  • Workout history in the App: Delete a workout in the App. On iOS the deletion syncs to your other devices through iCloud
  • Health records: A workout written to Apple Health or Health Connect is a separate copy; delete it in the Health app or in Health Connect. Deleting it there does not remove it from the App's history, and deleting it in the App does not remove it from your health store
  • iCloud copy (iOS): Deleting a workout in the App removes the synced copy once sync has run. Turning off iCloud for Canter stops future sync but does not delete what is already stored; remove that in Settings > Apple Account > iCloud > Manage Account Storage > Canter
  • Device backups (iOS): Your device's own backups are separate from the iCloud sync copy; deleting a synced workout does not touch them. To remove the App's data from iCloud Backup, go to Settings > Apple Account > iCloud > Manage Account Storage > Backups, choose the device, and turn Canter off; that deletes the App's backed-up data and keeps it out of future backups, while the rest of the device's backup stays. Deleting the whole device backup there removes every app's data, not only Canter's. A backup on a computer is deleted on that computer
  • Google backup (Android): The backup copy follows Google's backup settings on your device (Settings > Google > Backup). Those controls are device-wide: turning app backup off and deleting the backup removes the backup of every app on the device, not only the App's. A reinstall with backup on may restore your history
  • Usage and crash reports, and survey answers: Turning the switch off stops new reports at once. Deleting a workout in the App or uninstalling the App does not remove usage events, crash reports or survey answers already sent; they stay under the retention and deletion terms described under Usage and crash reports. Reports already sent carry only a random installation identifier, so we cannot look them up by name or account; the one exception is a survey answer whose exact text you give us. If you tell support that text, we can find the answer and the events under the same identifier and remove them as far as PostHog's deletion tools allow
  • Uninstalling: Removes the App's own storage on the device: your workout history, settings, plan progress and the random installation identifier used for usage and crash reports. On iOS, the App Check token and attestation record that Firebase keeps in the device keychain may survive an uninstall, as keychain entries do. It does not by itself remove your health records, your iCloud copy, your Google backup, or usage events, crash reports and survey answers already sent
  • What you sent us: A support email stays in our support mailbox so we can answer follow-ups; write to the same address and we will delete it. Feedback entered during a closed test on Google Play is shown to us in the Google Play Console and kept there under Google's rules; we do not copy it anywhere else
